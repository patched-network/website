## Linear (eg: books, movies)

One obvious (and pretty effective!) way to sequence content is in a line. Say, one page after another. This isn't exactly a *considered* approach, but the natual consequence of publishing in physical media.

Linear arrangements give authors a lot of control. Specifically, authors can actively manage the cumulative context that the reader (listener, watch) holds whenever they encounter a specifc material. Topic D can be introduced after prerequisite topics A, B and C have been treated. Topic D can include brief callbacks to the most pertinent ideas from A, B, C, etc.

This control is especially powerful and fun in fiction or artistic contexts. In the novel *A Spot of Bother*, narration alternates in each chapter between a father and his grown son. The reader encounters each character's frustrations with their relationship in the limited first-person perspective, but the reader always holds both perspectives simlutaneously. The climax of the book is a minor difference that escalates immediately into a blowout fight. The effect is striking: the causes of the blowout are transparent to the reader, and it feels inevitable. But the reader simultaneously understands that for each character there are no clear and charitable explanations for the incident - they've arrived at the spot of bother.

## Assortive Matching (eg: lichess puzzle matching)

Chess players enjoy a very simple measurement of skill called an ELO rating - a number that gets adjusted according to your record of wins and losses against other rated players. The short of it is that if you play a chess game against someone with a couple of hundred points of your rating, the game has a reasonable chance of going either way - a good match. Beyond, say, a 400 point differential, the lower rated player becomes something like the proverbial snowball in hell.
